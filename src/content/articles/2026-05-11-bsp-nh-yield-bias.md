Pick a random race code on a random day. Back every horse in the field at Betfair Starting Price. After 100,000 races, how do you finish?

It depends on the code.

On flat turf, you're down about 1.6%. On all-weather, down nearly 5%. On hurdles, up almost 11%. On National Hunt flat (bumpers), bang on zero.

On chases? **Up 26.3%.**

That number is across 24,077 settled chase events between January 2023 and April 2026 — backed blind at Betfair SP, after the 2% exchange commission. Not picks. Not selections. Every horse, every chase, every day.

This isn't a quirk of a small sample, and it isn't selection skill. It's a structural feature of how chase markets clear on the exchange — and once you've seen it, you can't read a chase tipster's ROI the same way again.

## The per-code picture

Here's the baseline — the return from backing every settled runner at BSP over the full 2023-2026 window:

| Race code | Bets | Strike rate | Back-everything ROI |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Flat turf | 100,625 | 11.17% | **−1.63%** |
| All-weather flat | 84,343 | 11.03% | **−4.82%** |
| Hurdles | 50,357 | 13.25% | **+10.78%** |
| National Hunt flat | 7,319 | 11.22% | +0.06% |
| **Chases** | **24,077** | **17.44%** | **+26.33%** |

Flat and all-weather behave the way you'd expect a near-efficient market to behave: the exchange grinds slightly negative once commission is taken out, but not by much. Bumpers are essentially fair. Hurdles tilt positive. Chases tilt sharply positive.

Why the asymmetry?

## Why the chase market settles wide

Three things are happening at once.

**1. BSP is volume-weighted, not fair-weighted.** Betfair's Starting Price is calculated from the matched bets in the closing seconds before the off. It is not a model's "true" price — it's whatever the market cleared at. If buyers and sellers settle the favourite at a thin price and leave the longshots alone, the published SP reflects that exactly.

**2. Recreational money concentrates on favourites.** Most NH bettors are recreational, and recreational bettors back the proven runner. That means the favourite's BSP is pulled tight to (or sometimes below) fair odds, because the volume is there to clear it. The mid-runners and longshots, meanwhile, don't attract that volume — so their prices stay elevated above fair.

**3. There aren't enough sharps to arbitrage it back.** On flat turf and all-weather, exchange trading bots and professional arbitrage desks continuously push prices toward fair. There's enough size in those markets to make that work. On chases, the trading population is thinner. The longshot premium doesn't get competed away.

The net effect is that when you back every runner — not just the favourite — you collect the longshot premium that recreational backers never bid out. The favourite costs you a touch; the rest of the field pays for it and more.

The pattern is most extreme on chases because chases are the thinnest NH market by some distance. Hurdles get hit by the same dynamic but less severely. Bumpers vanish into the noise because nobody's volume is sustained.

## Seven years, one direction

You might suspect this is a recent anomaly. It isn't. Here's the chase baseline broken out by year, going back to 2020:

| Year | Settled chases | Strike rate | Back-everything ROI |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 2020 | 6,090 | 16.17% | +36.05% |
| 2021 | 7,973 | 16.76% | +23.22% |
| 2022 | 7,413 | 18.20% | +30.92% |
| 2023 | 7,275 | 17.62% | +23.87% |
| 2024 | 7,321 | 16.83% | +27.26% |
| 2025 | 6,765 | 17.49% | +23.59% |
| 2026 (Jan–Apr) | 2,716 | 18.45% | +37.23% |

Every year, positive. Lowest +23%. Highest +37%. No year close to zero, no year underwater. Seven independent samples of several thousand bets each, all pointing the same way. That isn't variance — that's a feature of the market.

It's worth saying this is also stable across the major rule changes the industry has been through — premium-charge thresholds tightened, commission rates moved, the BHA reshuffled the fixture list, going-stick standardisation came and went. The chase baseline didn't move.

## What this means for how you read chase tipster numbers

Here's the part that should change how you read every chase-tipping service in the country, ours included.

If a service publishes a +40% ROI on chases, your first instinct is to be impressed. Compare it to a benchmark of zero — most ROI numbers are framed as "did the service make money or not?" — and +40% looks like a real edge.

But the benchmark isn't zero on chases. It's +26%.

A chase angle returning +40% is contributing 14 percentage points of selection skill over what you'd get by backing every horse. A chase angle returning +30% is contributing 4. A chase angle returning +20% — despite a respectable-looking headline — is *underperforming the baseline*. A service publishing +20% chase ROI isn't beating the market. It's worse than backing every runner blind.

Compare to flat. A flat angle returning +30% is delivering 31.6 percentage points of selection lift against a −1.6% baseline. That's three times the real edge in a number that looks the same on paper.

This is why a single ROI figure is misleading without a baseline reference. And it's why we are changing how we publish our angle numbers — see the last section.

## The operational catches

Before we get to that, three things worth saying about the chase baseline as a "product" in its own right. The temptation, looking at the table above, is to say "great, just back every chaser." That's almost right — but it comes with three caveats that anyone considering it needs to take seriously.

**1. Turnover and drawdown.** A typical chase has seven runners. Backing every one of them at one unit a piece is seven units of stake per race. Across a year's chase calendar — roughly 1,000 events — that's 7,000 units staked for an expected return of about 1,800 units. The expected profit is excellent, but the gross flow is large, and so is the variance around it. Single-month drawdowns of 50 to 100 units are routine even at the +26% expectation. This is real money in motion, and the bankroll discipline that comes with it isn't optional.

**2. Liquidity at the off.** The BSP figure is computed from the bets that actually matched in the closing seconds. It does not guarantee that *your* bet would have filled at that price at any size. Thin chase markets clear small back orders cleanly; £100-and-up stakes can clear available volume on the longshots and shift the published number. The +26.33% theoretical doesn't fully transfer to large stakes — paper ROI and realised ROI start to diverge once you scale up.

**3. Premium and Patriot charges.** At commercial volumes, Betfair's Premium and Patriot charges start to deduct from net winnings. The headline figure on this page is gross of those charges. Sustained winners at high turnover should expect material erosion — historically the Premium Charge has run at 20% of profit, with a higher tier on the largest accounts. At small recreational stakes the headline survives intact. At commercial scale, it doesn't.

The chase baseline is a real thing. It is not a turnkey strategy.

## What changes from here

We're reporting all of our chase angle ROIs as **lift over baseline** going forward.

A chase angle on the ledger or in the briefing will be headlined with its percentage-point lift — the genuine selection edge — rather than its raw return. The raw number stays available for anyone who wants to inspect it, but the headline figure is the contribution above the structural baseline. That means our chase numbers become directly comparable with our flat and all-weather numbers: across all four codes, the headline tells you the selection skill in real units. Apples to apples.

This is the honest way to publish performance on a market where the do-nothing yield is materially non-zero. It also keeps us honest with ourselves — we won't be tempted to lean on the chase baseline as if it were our edge. Backing every chaser at SP is profitable on average, but it's a feature of the market, not of us.

The chase baseline has been there for seven years. We aren't going to double-count it.
