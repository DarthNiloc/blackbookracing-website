A few weeks ago we published the chase-market baseline piece — backing
every horse in every chase at Betfair SP pays +26.3% per year, every
year. That's not selection skill; it's a structural feature of the way
thinly-traded NH markets settle.

The opposite end of the spectrum is just as interesting. Back every
horse in every flat race at Betfair SP and you finish slightly down.
Back every horse on all-weather flat and you finish a bit further down.
Bumpers come in roughly at zero. The picture across the five codes:

| Race code | Bets | Strike rate | Back-everything ROI |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| **Flat turf** | **100,625** | **11.17%** | **−1.63%** |
| **All-weather flat** | **84,343** | **11.03%** | **−4.82%** |
| Hurdles | 50,357 | 13.25% | +10.78% |
| National Hunt flat | 7,319 | 11.22% | +0.06% |
| Chases | 24,077 | 17.44% | +26.33% |

Flat and all-weather sit right at the floor. The exchange takes 2%
commission on winners. After that, the residual edge from backing the
field blind is essentially zero on flat (−1.6%) and only slightly worse
on all-weather (−4.8%). Compare to chases at +26.3% gross — a 28
percentage-point gap between the deepest market and the thinnest.

That's not coincidence. It's a structural feature of how exchange
markets clear under different liquidity conditions, and it changes how
any flat-racing tipster's numbers should be read.

## Why flat markets settle close to fair

Three things working together explain the −1.6% / −4.8% baselines:

**1. Deep liquidity attracts sharp money.** Flat turf is the headline
UK racing code by volume — most race-day betting traffic, most exchange
matched volume, most professional-trader and arbitrage-bot participation.
On any given race, the price you see at the off has been pushed and
pulled by far more sharp pressure than a 7-runner chase market gets.
Anytime a mid-price horse is wider than fair, someone with size lays it
back into shape.

**2. Bookmaker overrounds are tight on flat.** UK bookmakers price
flat-turf markets aggressively — overrounds in the 105-110% range are
typical for high-profile cards. That tight pricing leaks into the
exchange via the small army of traders who arbitrage book vs. exchange
in the closing minutes. The market's view-of-fair gets continuously
refined.

**3. Recreational money is more evenly spread.** Flat-racing punters
back longshots, mid-prices, and favourites in patterns that look much
closer to the implied probability distribution. On chases, recreational
money concentrates heavily on the proven favourite — that asymmetric
flow is exactly what leaves the longshot premium unbid. On flat, the
asymmetry is much less pronounced.

The combination of these three forces drives flat BSP toward fair. The
2% commission is most of what's left when you back blind across the
field — and the marginal extra −0.4pp on flat (and −2.8pp on all-weather)
is the residual market drift after sharp pressure has done its work.

## Seven years of nothing changing

It's worth showing the year-by-year picture so you can see this isn't
a one-window artefact.

| Year | FLT bets | FLT back-all ROI | AWF bets | AWF back-all ROI |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 2020 | 24,692 | −0.99% | 23,736 | −7.90% |
| 2021 | 33,813 | −2.34% | 23,952 | −6.27% |
| 2022 | 32,422 | +2.28% | 23,587 | −9.78% |
| 2023 | 32,666 | −1.30% | 24,598 | −4.87% |
| 2024 | 32,925 | −3.15% | 25,578 | −5.09% |
| 2025 | 32,276 | −0.46% | 24,740 | −5.00% |
| 2026 (Jan–Apr) | 2,758 | −1.12% | 9,427 | −3.49% |

Flat hovers within a tight band of zero, year after year — the +2.28%
in 2022 is the only year noticeably above zero, and is itself within
sampling noise on n=32,422. All-weather is consistently worse — never
positive, settling around −5% to −7%. Both behave like efficient markets
should: small, persistent, near-the-cost-of-trading edges to the bookmaker
and exchange.

## Why all-weather is slightly worse than turf

The 3-point gap between flat (−1.6%) and all-weather (−4.8%) deserves
a brief note. All-weather racing gets less media attention, less
sophisticated trader participation, and lower exchange liquidity. The
markets are still mostly efficient — there's no chase-style longshot
premium — but the residual inefficiency tilts a touch the wrong way:
favourites are slightly over-priced (mildly under-favouriting), which
when you back the whole field shows up as a slightly worse ROI.

Practically, this means all-weather is the closest thing flat racing
has to "harder than fair" — your tipster needs to overcome a slightly
deeper structural headwind to publish a positive ROI on AWF picks
compared to FLT picks.

## What this means for how you read flat tipster numbers

This is the inverse of the chase-baseline story, and the implications
are the inverse too.

On chases, a tipster publishing a +40% ROI is contributing only 14
percentage points of actual selection skill over the +26.3% chase
baseline. The number sounds impressive, but most of it is the market.

On flat, a tipster publishing a +20% ROI is contributing **21.6
percentage points of selection skill** over the −1.6% flat baseline.
The same headline number means something dramatically different:

- A +20% chase angle: −6pp below baseline. Below the do-nothing rate.
- A +20% flat angle: +21.6pp above baseline. Real edge.

A +10% flat angle is +11.6pp of selection skill. Even a +5% flat ROI
beats the market by 6.6pp — modest but genuine.

This is why we publish flat ROI numbers as raw figures rather than
lift-over-baseline. The flat baseline is so close to zero that the raw
number *is* the selection skill, within commission noise. We don't need
a baseline correction for FLT or AWF picks. The number says what it
seems to say.

On chases we will, going forward, lead with lift-over-baseline numbers
rather than raw ROI — because on chases the raw number is misleading.
The flat numbers stay raw. The framing is consistent if you read both
as "selection skill in percentage points over a baseline of zero" —
which is what they already are, on flat.

## What this changes about a flat tipster's claims

If you're evaluating any flat-racing tipster service (ours, anyone
else's), a useful sanity check is to compare their published ROI to the
flat baseline you now know:

- **Positive flat ROI is real edge.** There's no structural drift to
  hide behind. Even +5% flat is the tipster having genuinely picked
  winners faster than commission ate them.
- **Zero or slightly-positive flat ROI is uninteresting.** It means the
  tipster is roughly equivalent to backing the field blind, which is
  itself slightly negative. Pay for that, and you're paying for noise.
- **Negative flat ROI means the picks are worse than random.** This
  happens — selection-skill claims can fail to deliver even against an
  efficient market. Useful to know.
- **Same logic, harsher floor, on all-weather.** AWF tipsters need to
  clear a −4.8% structural drag plus commission before showing positive
  ROI. A +5% AWF ROI is a +9.8pp genuine selection lift over the AWF
  baseline.

## A note on what doesn't change

The operational catches we mentioned for chase markets — drawdown
variance, BSP fill at retail size, Premium Charge at scale — all still
apply to flat and AWF betting. None of them are specific to the chase
baseline finding; they're general features of running a Betfair-anchored
tipster operation at volume. Worth a brief re-read if you didn't catch
those in the chase-baseline piece.

## Putting both pieces together

The two articles together describe a continuum:

- **Chase markets** are the thinly-traded, recreational-money-dominated
  end. Big structural baseline (+26.3%). A chase angle's headline ROI
  needs to be heavily discounted to see the real selection edge.
- **Hurdle markets** are intermediate — moderately liquid, partial
  longshot premium (+10.8% baseline). Hurdle angles need a smaller but
  real baseline adjustment.
- **Flat turf and all-weather** are deep, near-efficient. Their
  baselines sit close to or just below commission. Selection skill on
  these markets is harder to come by but, when present, is fully
  reflected in the headline number.

This is what we mean when we say we report performance against the
right yardstick for each market. The same percentage point of ROI is
worth very different things across race codes, and from now on we'll
make sure the framing matches the market.
